Exoplanet Hunt

Find a real planet, hiding in real starlight.

Most planets we know about around other stars were found this way: not by seeing them, but by watching their star's light dim for a few hours, over and over, on a steady beat. NASA's TESS telescope stares at one patch of sky for about a month at a time and records the brightness of every star in it, every two minutes. Below is real TESS data for three real stars. You'll walk through the same five steps astronomers use to pull a planet out of the noise: clean the data, search for the beat, fold it on that rhythm, then measure the planet from what the dip tells you.

1 The raw light curve

This is what TESS actually recorded: the star's brightness, point by point, for about a month. Every dot is one measurement. Can you spot the planet by eye? Probably not yet. There's real noise here, and the star's own brightness drifts a little on its own, which has nothing to do with a planet.

2 Detrend: flatten out the drift

Stars flicker and drift for reasons that have nothing to do with planets: starspots rotating in and out of view, or the telescope itself settling slightly. We flatten this out with a moving median, a smooth line that tracks the slow drift but ignores quick dips. Dividing the data by that line leaves the flattened flux, which is what we search next.

3 Search: find the repeating beat

A transit happens once every orbit, like clockwork. If we guess the right repeat length (the period) and fold every orbit on top of the one before it, all the transits stack up into one clean dip. Guess wrong, and they scatter and cancel out. The search below, called Box Least Squares (BLS), tries thousands of candidate periods and scores each one by how deep and clean a dip it finds when folded that way. Here the flattened data is folded on the period you picked, every orbit lined up on top of the others. Why this works

A planet's own light is far too faint to see next to its star, so we don't look for the planet. We look for its shadow. As it crosses in front of the star, it blocks a small, predictable fraction of the star's light, for a few hours, once every orbit. That fraction depends only on how big the planet looks against the star: (planet radius / star radius)². A period this regular, this clean, and this short, is the signature a transiting planet leaves behind.

Limits: a dip alone doesn't prove a planet

A regular dip is strong evidence, but not proof by itself. A small star eclipsing a bigger one (an eclipsing binary) can fake a shallow, "V" shaped dip that looks similar. So can a faint background star blended into the same pixel. Real confirmation needs more: a flat-bottomed "U" shaped dip of consistent depth, a mass measurement from the star's wobble (radial velocity), or a repeat with a different telescope. Every planet in this page was confirmed that way, not from TESS data alone.
